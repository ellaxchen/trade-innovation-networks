What happened in 2010?

Nothing in the real world. The database started seeing China.

Layer 1 — EPO's database didn't contain most Chinese documents

PATSTAT is the European Patent Office's worldwide patent database, compiled from documents contributed by every national patent office. Citations are recorded from patent examiners' search reports — but a citation can only appear in the data if the document involved is in the database.

Before ~2009–2010, EPO had indexed only a thin sliver of Chinese patent office (CNIPA) publications. So a Chinese patent citing anything — or being cited — was mostly invisible. Not absent. Unrecorded.

Layer 2 — the family-level data could see China through a side door

Patents are territorial. A Chinese company protecting an invention abroad files the same invention again at the US, Japanese, or European office. The patent system calls this set of filings a family — one invention, several official registrations.

Before 2010, 25.2% of Chinese inventions were also filed in a foreign office — and those foreign copies were in EPO's database all along. Counting at the family level (one node per invention, whichever office was indexed) therefore sees China's knowledge activity before 2010. Counting at the application level (one node per document) sees almost nothing.

Layer 3 — the cross-border share betrays the sample selection

The slice of China that was visible before 2010 (the internationally-filed inventions) cites foreign knowledge heavily. When the China-only majority became visible in 2010, it mostly cited other Chinese patents — diluting the cross-border share:

The book analogy

You write a book and publish it in two countries:

  • The Chinese edition — sold only in China
  • The English edition — same book, translated, sold in the US

A European library has a rule: "We only catalog English books." The Chinese edition sits on the shelf, but the library has no record of it. If someone cites your book, the library only counts the citation when it points to the English edition.

Answers to the three questions

Q: So there were no Chinese citation records before 2010?

A: Not literally zero — a few hundred per year (155 in 2005, 2,148 in 2009). That's about 0.1% of the post-2010 level. Effectively invisible, not absolutely absent.

Q: What is the EPO database?

A: PATSTAT — the European Patent Office's worldwide patent database. Every national patent office (US, JP, CN, …) contributes its documents, and EPO assembles them into linked tables (applications, publications, citations, families). The citations come from patent examiners' search reports. If a document isn't in the database, the citations attached to it don't exist in the data. Coverage of each country depends on what that office shares with EPO and how quickly EPO processes it — Chinese documents only became comprehensively integrated around 2010.

Q: Did all Chinese inventions get filed twice?

A: No. Only 25.2% were also filed in a foreign office. The other 74.8% were China-only. Filing abroad costs real money — translation, examination fees, agents — so only inventions with commercial value outside China go abroad. Most domestic Chinese inventions stayed domestic, and stayed invisible to the data.

Bottom line

China was citing 100,000–250,000 times a year all along — the family-level floor proves it. The 2010 "explosion" is EPO coverage completion, not an innovation surge.

Even family-level data underestimates pre-2010 China by roughly 4× — only the 25% internationally-filed inventions were visible. The honest answer to "when did China's knowledge centrality start rising" is: before our data can see it.

Any app-level time series (including the old lead/lag finding of "China trade-first at +5 years, r = 0.96") treats this discontinuity as real growth. It isn't. The family-level lead/lag shows the opposite: knowledge leads trade by 5 years at r = 0.990.
